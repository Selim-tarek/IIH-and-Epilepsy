# ABSTRACT

Objective: To estimate the rate of incident seizures and epilepsy after
idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) and to examine whether
differential healthcare surveillance could account for the association.

Methods: We conducted a retrospective matched cohort study using
electronic health records from Mayo Clinic. Adolescents and adults with IIH
(aged 13-59 years), identified by an IIH diagnosis code and lumbar puncture,
were matched to up to four
comparators without IIH on sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and BMI
calendar year. Comparators inherited the index date of their matched
case. After a 180-day washout to exclude prevalent seizure or epilepsy
diagnoses and chronic antiseizure medication use, the primary outcome
was a clinically recognized seizure or epilepsy event identified by
diagnosis code or sustained antiseizure medication use. Hazard ratios
(HRs) were estimated with Cox regression using robust variance clustered
by matched set. We evaluated differential detection using
negative-control outcomes, empirical calibration, adjustment for
pre-index healthcare contact, post-index surveillance rates, and
E-values.

Results: The analytic cohort included 2,138 patients with IIH and 5,743
comparators, contributing 13,938 person-years of follow-up. Incident
seizure or epilepsy occurred at 14.46 versus 6.72 events per 1,000
person-years (HR 2.28, 95% confidence interval \[CI\] 1.62--3.22).
Three-year cumulative incidence was 3.83% versus 1.71%. The HR was 2.30
(95% CI 1.35--3.92) when restricted to epilepsy-specific codes and 1.93
(95% CI 1.34--2.77) when the medication criterion was removed. Estimates
remained similar in landmark analyses and after adjustment for
obstructive sleep apnea, hypertension, and polycystic ovary syndrome.
Healthcare contact was substantially greater among patients with IIH,
and all 11 negative-control outcomes were more prevalent at baseline.
Adjustment for pre-index healthcare contact moved negative-control
estimates toward the null but did not attenuate the seizure association.
Empirical calibration estimated a detection-attributable HR of 1.04, although its interval was wide and included the observed estimate.

Significance: In this electronic health record cohort, IIH was
associated with approximately twice the rate of incident seizure or
epilepsy, although the absolute excess was modest. Differential
surveillance was present and could not be excluded as a partial contributor,
but several analyses argue against it explaining the association in full. The findings support further study of seizure phenotypes
and potential structural mechanisms, particularly skull-base
encephaloceles, in patients with IIH.

*Keywords: idiopathic intracranial hypertension; seizures; epilepsy;
cohort study; electronic health records; healthcare surveillance*

# KEY POINTS

• Patients with IIH had approximately twice the rate of incident seizure
or epilepsy as matched comparators.

• The absolute excess was modest: 3-year cumulative incidence was 3.83%
with IIH and 1.71% without it.

• Healthcare contact was substantially greater among patients with IIH,
demonstrating an important risk of differential ascertainment.

• The association persisted across every outcome definition, cohort restriction,
landmark and analytic model (range 1.93-2.79).

• Adjustment for pre-index healthcare contact moved all 66 negative-control
estimates toward the null, while the seizure estimate moved away from it under
all six measures.

• Empirical calibration did not exclude detection bias: its interval for a
detection-only effect included the observed estimate.

• Future studies should characterize seizure phenotype and skull-base
encephaloceles prospectively.


























# 1 \| INTRODUCTION

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a disorder characterized by
elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure in the absence of an identifiable
secondary cause. It occurs predominantly in women of reproductive age with
obesity, and its incidence continues to rise in parallel with the global obesity
epidemic.1 According to the revised Friedman criteria, IIH is defined by
papilledema or abducens nerve palsy, elevated lumbar puncture opening pressure,
normal CSF composition, a normal neurologic examination apart from cranial nerve
abnormalities, and neuroimaging demonstrating no space-occupying lesion,
hydrocephalus, or cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.2 A normal mental status and
the absence of focal neurologic deficits are themselves diagnostic requirements.
Natural-history studies describe headache, transient visual obscurations,
pulsatile tinnitus, visual loss, and diplopia as the characteristic clinical
spectrum of IIH, without an established association with seizures or epilepsy
either at presentation or during long-term follow-up.1,3,4,5

This distinction has important diagnostic implications as IIH remains a diagnosis
of exclusion. Among the most important mimics is cerebral venous thrombosis,
which can reproduce the headache, papilledema, and sixth-nerve palsy
characteristic of IIH but frequently presents with seizures or focal neurologic
deficits due to cortical venous involvement.6,7 Consequently, diagnostic
paradigms have long distinguished pressure-mediated disease without seizures from
structural venous pathology in which seizures are common. This leads to the view
that epilepsy represents evidence against rather than a consequence of IIH.

However, an indirect mechanistic link between IIH and epilepsy has been proposed.
Chronic intracranial hypertension may lead to skull-base remodeling and osseous
erosion, resulting in meningoceles and, most notably, temporal lobe
encephaloceles through the middle cranial fossa. These structural abnormalities
have been described in patients with IIH, spontaneous skull-base CSF leaks, and
drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy, suggesting that sustained elevations in
intracranial pressure could create a substrate for epileptogenesis over time
rather than precipitating seizures acutely.8 Whether this anatomic association
translates into an increased population-level risk of incident seizures or
epilepsy remains unknown. Current prognostic frameworks for IIH therefore remain
focused on visual outcomes, headache burden, and disease recurrence, with little
consideration of seizure-related outcomes.9,10

We conducted a matched retrospective cohort study to determine whether IIH is
associated with an increased risk of incident seizures or epilepsy compared with
matched individuals without IIH. We additionally sought to characterize the
magnitude and temporal pattern of any observed association and to evaluate its
robustness using a prespecified negative-control framework designed to assess
potential differential ascertainment bias.

# 2 \| METHODS

## 2.1 \| Study design, data source, and ethics

This retrospective matched cohort study used the integrated electronic
health record of Mayo Clinic (EHR), covering Mayo Clinic sites in
Minnesota, Florida, and Arizona. The record contains inpatient,
outpatient, emergency, and procedural encounters, together with linked
diagnosis codes, problem lists, medication records, flowsheet vital
signs, and vital status. Index dates ranged from June 7, 2002, through
November 11, 2025, and the data were frozen on September 3, 2026.

This report follows the STROBE and RECORD reporting guidelines.11,12 The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board,
which waived the requirement for informed consent.

## 2.2 \| Study population

Patients with IIH. Adolescents and adults were identified using an IIH
diagnosis code (ICD-9-CM 348.2 or ICD-10-CM G93.2, in any position)
together with a lumbar puncture record (CPT 62270 or 62328, or an
electronic lumbar puncture procedure entry). The index date was the date
of the diagnostic lumbar puncture. IIH diagnoses were not adjudicated
against the revised diagnostic criteria; opening pressure was abstracted
when available.2 The complete code list is provided in eTable
1. Comparators were patients with no IIH diagnosis code at any time in
the available record.

Eligibility criteria. Patients with a history of ischemic stroke, intracerebral
or subarachnoid hemorrhage, craniotomy, brain tumor, or traumatic brain injury
were excluded from the comparator group, because these conditions are
independent risk factors for seizures. This exclusion was applied at the stage
of comparator extraction and **was not applied to the IIH group**, for whom the
corresponding diagnosis extract was not available. The asymmetry is therefore a
known deviation from the intended design rather than an analytic choice, and its
expected direction is to remove seizure-prone individuals from the comparator
group only, which would bias the hazard ratio away from the null. It is
quantified no further here because the data needed to apply the same exclusion
to the IIH group were not obtainable; a symmetric re-analysis is the first
priority for any future revision.

Both groups
required at least one dated clinical encounter during the 12 months
before the index date. Clinical encounters were distinguished from
administrative activity (messages, orders, refills, and record
maintenance) using an encounter-type list applied identically to both
groups; approximately 60% of encounter rows were administrative.

Patients with a qualifying seizure diagnosis or chronic antiseizure
medication (ASM) use on or before the end of the 180-day washout were
excluded. Patients without follow-up beyond the washout were also
excluded. All analyses were repeated using an alternative definition in
which every encounter row was treated as a contact.

## 2.3 \| Index date and matching

Each comparator inherited the index date of its matched case.
Eligibility, washout, exclusion of prevalent seizures, covariate
measurement, and the beginning of follow-up were therefore anchored to
the same calendar date within each matched set. Matching was performed
without replacement on sex, age, BMI, and BMI calendar year (within 2
years), at a variable ratio of up to 1:4.

Matching used four sequential passes through a randomly ordered case list, with
five prespecified tiers of decreasing stringency: (1) race, BMI calendar year,
age within 3 years and BMI within 3 kg/m²; (2) the same without race; (3) the
same without BMI calendar year; (4) age within 3 years and BMI within 5 kg/m²;
and (5) age within 5 years and BMI within 5 kg/m². Race was specified but was
unrecorded for every patient with IIH and therefore never constrained the match. BMI was taken from the flowsheet measurement closest in calendar
time to the index date.

## 2.4 \| Outcomes

The primary outcome was incident seizure or epilepsy after the 180-day
washout, defined by a single rule applied to both groups. The first
criterion was a qualifying diagnosis code in the diagnosis record or
problem list: ICD-10-CM G40.x or R56.x, or ICD-9-CM 345.x or 780.39. The
second criterion was an ASM prescription or medication record indicating
treatment continued for at least 180 days.

Febrile, post-traumatic, psychogenic nonepileptic or conversion
seizures, family history of seizures, migraine diagnoses, and ASM
adverse-effect codes were not considered qualifying outcomes. Topiramate
and acetazolamide were not counted as ASMs because they are commonly
used to treat IIH and have antiseizure activity. Gabapentin, pregabalin,
and benzodiazepines were also excluded because they have common
analgesic and procedural indications. The event date was the earlier of
the first qualifying diagnosis and the first qualifying medication
record.

Because the EHR did not reliably permit classification by seizure type
or by provoked versus unprovoked status, the primary outcome represents
a clinically recognized seizure or epilepsy event rather than epilepsy
as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE).13
Epilepsy-specific codes (G40.x/345.x) were analyzed separately as a more
specific approximation of epilepsy.

Among patients who experienced a primary outcome, a secondary outcome
classified events as recurrent seizure or epilepsy if there was an
epilepsy-specific diagnosis code, two or more documented seizure events,
or indefinite ASM therapy.

Outcome ascertainment was evaluated by manual chart review of a random sample of 50 patients with IIH and 50 comparators who met the electronic outcome definition. Positive predictive value (PPV) was calculated as
the proportion of electronically identified events confirmed as clinical
seizures, with exact binomial 95% confidence intervals.

## 2.5 \| Follow-up and covariates

Follow-up began at the end of the 180-day washout and ended at the
earliest of the primary outcome, death, the last attended clinical
encounter, the data-freeze date, or 3 years. OSA, hypertension,
polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and bariatric surgery were identified
from diagnosis or procedure codes recorded on or before the index date.
Pre-index healthcare contact was measured as the number of clinical
encounters during the preceding 12 months.

The use of last attended clinical encounter as a censoring date was
intended to avoid attributing person-time to individuals no longer
observable in the health system. Because healthcare contact is itself
related to the detection process, however, informative censoring is a
potential source of bias and was considered in interpretation.

## 2.6 \| Statistical analysis

Incidence rates are reported per 1,000 person-years with exact Poisson
95% confidence intervals. Cause-specific hazard ratios (HRs) were
estimated using Cox regression with exposure as the sole model term
because age, sex, and BMI were addressed through matching. Variance was
estimated using a robust sandwich estimator clustered by matched set.14
Proportional hazards were assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals.
Cumulative incidence was estimated using the Aalen--Johansen estimator
with death as a competing event, and a Fine--Gray model was used to
estimate the corresponding subdistribution HR.15 All tests were
two-sided.

Prespecified sensitivity analyses included: (1) diagnosis codes without
the medication criterion; (2) epilepsy-specific codes only; (3)
restriction to IIH cases with opening pressure of at least 25 cm H₂O and
their matched comparators; (4) counting all encounter rows as healthcare
contact; (5) landmark analyses excluding events occurring during the
first 30, 90, or 180 days after the washout; (6) overlap weighting16; and
(7) restriction to complete matched sets.

The overlap-weighting propensity model included age, sex, BMI, and the
logarithm of pre-index visits. Effect modification by age, BMI, and sex
was assessed using Wald tests for interaction. OSA, PCOS, and
hypertension remained imbalanced after matching; therefore, a
comorbidity-adjusted analysis was performed after the primary analysis.
This analysis was prespecified for interpretation after observing the
post-matching imbalance and was reported regardless of direction of
effect.

A covariate was considered a potential confounder when it was both
imbalanced after matching (absolute SMD \>0.10) and associated with the
outcome. Analyses were performed in R.

## 2.7 \| Assessment of differential detection

We used five complementary approaches to evaluate differential
ascertainment.

First, we examined 11 negative-control outcomes without a plausible
direct causal relationship to IIH or seizures, selected to be
ascertainable through similar healthcare pathways (eTable 3).17 We did not
treat a negative control as evidence of specificity unless its baseline
prevalence was reasonably balanced between groups.

Second, we performed an empirical calibration analysis in which the log
HR for each negative-control outcome was regressed on its log baseline
prevalence ratio. The fitted value at a baseline prevalence ratio of 1
was used to estimate the association expected from the observed pattern
of differential detection alone.18

Third, we adjusted each outcome for six prespecified measures of
pre-index healthcare contact and examined the direction and magnitude of
change. If differential healthcare use were an important source of
ascertainment bias, adjustment for these measures would be expected,
although not guaranteed, to attenuate the observed associations.

Fourth, we described post-index surveillance rates. We did not adjust
the primary outcome for post-index contact because post-exposure
healthcare contact may lie on the pathway from IIH to outcome
ascertainment and can act as a collider.

Fifth, we calculated E-values to describe the strength of association
that an unmeasured confounder would need with both IIH and the outcome,
conditional on the measured covariates, to explain the observed HR.19
Because healthcare-contact measures do not directly quantify their
association with seizure ascertainment, E-values were interpreted as
sensitivity parameters rather than as direct tests of detection bias.

# 3 \| RESULTS

## 3.1 \| Cohort and baseline characteristics

Among 2,520 eligible patients with IIH, 2,490 (98.8%) were matched to
5,743 comparators, yielding a mean of 2.31 comparators per case.
Eighty-seven percent of cases were matched at the most stringent tier.
After the washout and follow-up eligibility criteria were applied, 2,138
patients with IIH contributed to the primary analysis. The IIH and
comparator groups contributed 4,563.0 and 9,375.2 person-years,
respectively. Median follow-up was 2.77 years (IQR 1.23--3.00) in the
IIH group and 1.51 years (IQR 0.52--3.00) among comparators.

Age, sex, and BMI were well balanced (SMDs −0.065, 0.034, and 0.050,
respectively; Table 1). OSA remained more common among patients with IIH
(29.5% vs 15.4%; SMD 0.342), as did PCOS (12.3% vs 7.3%; SMD 0.171) and
hypertension (22.1% vs 16.4%; SMD 0.146). Pre-index healthcare contact
showed the largest imbalance: the median number of clinical visits
during the preceding year was 12 in the IIH group versus 4 among
comparators (SMD 0.526). Opening pressure was available for 1,585
patients with IIH (74%) and had a mean of 29.5 cm H₂O (SD 9.7).

## 3.2 \| Primary outcome

During follow-up, 66 patients with IIH and 63 comparators experienced a
qualifying seizure or epilepsy event. Incidence rates were 14.46 and
6.72 per 1,000 person-years, respectively (95% CIs 11.19--18.40 and
5.16--8.60). The corresponding HR was 2.28 (95% CI 1.62--3.22;
p\<0.001), with no evidence of violation of the proportional hazards
assumption (p=0.94). Three-year cumulative incidence was 3.83% in the
IIH group and 1.71% among comparators, an absolute difference of 2.12
percentage points.

In chart review, 48 of 50 electronically identified events among
patients with IIH were confirmed (PPV 96%; 95% CI 86%--100%), compared
with 50 of 50 among comparators (PPV 100%; 95% CI 93%--100%).

## 3.3 \| Sensitivity analyses

The association was similar across alternative outcome and cohort
definitions (Table 2; eFigure 1). Removing the medication criterion
produced an HR of 1.93 (95% CI 1.34--2.77), while restriction to
epilepsy-specific codes produced an HR of 2.30 (95% CI 1.35--3.92).
Restriction to patients with opening pressure of at least 25 cm H₂O
yielded an HR of 2.25 (95% CI 1.41--3.59). Landmark analyses excluding
the first 30, 90, and 180 days after the washout yielded HRs of 2.49,
2.29, and 2.22, respectively. The overlap-weighted analysis produced an
HR of 2.31 (95% CI 1.56--3.43). Counting all encounter rows as
healthcare contact produced an HR of 2.79 (95% CI 1.98--3.93).

Selection of follow-up was asymmetric in one respect: comparators, but
not cases, were screened for post-washout follow-up at selection.
Consequently, 352 matched sets lost their case but retained 789
comparators, who contributed two events. Restriction to complete matched
sets yielded an HR of 2.06 (95% CI 1.46--2.92).

Among the three imbalanced comorbidities, OSA showed the clearest
association with the outcome (HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.29--2.71); estimates for
hypertension and PCOS were 1.41 (95% CI 0.95--2.08) and 1.14 (95% CI
0.64--2.02), respectively. Adjustment for all three comorbidities
reduced the HR to 2.11 (95% CI 1.48--3.01), with the change attributable
primarily to OSA. Additional adjustment for pre-index visits yielded an
HR of 2.33 (95% CI 1.58--3.45). The cause-specific HR for death was 0.61
(95% CI 0.29--1.29), and the Fine--Gray subdistribution HR for the
primary outcome was 2.28 (95% CI 1.62--3.22).

## 3.4 \| Secondary outcome and subgroups

Among patients with a primary event, 41 of 66 patients with IIH (62%;
95% CI 49%--74%) and 32 of 63 comparators (51%; 95% CI 38%--64%) met the
definition of recurrent seizure or epilepsy (Fisher exact p=0.22;
eFigure 2). No interaction reached statistical significance (eTable 7).
HRs were 1.90 among patients younger than 35 years and 2.72 among those
aged 35 years or older (p for interaction=0.31). By BMI, HRs were 2.86
for BMI \<35 kg/m² and 1.87 for BMI ≥35 kg/m² (p=0.26). By sex, the HR
was 1.98 among women and 5.12 among men (p=0.055); the male estimate was
based on 20 events.

## 3.5 \| Differential detection

Healthcare contact after the index date was consistently higher among
patients with IIH (eTable 8). Rate ratios were 2.70 (95% CI 2.68--2.73)
for all clinical visits, 2.27--2.92 across office, procedural, and
hospital encounters, and 5.74 (95% CI 4.96--6.65) for emergency
department encounters. Laboratory contact was similar between groups
(rate ratio 1.14). Neurology visits, neuroimaging, and EEG could not be
compared because these data were not captured comparably in the two
source extracts.

All 11 negative-control outcomes were more prevalent at baseline among
patients with IIH, with prevalence ratios ranging from 1.6 to 5.0 (all
p\<0.01). Thus, none met the prespecified balance criterion for a
specificity analysis (Table 3). After the index date, 8 of 11 negative
controls had HRs whose 95% CIs excluded 1. The seizure outcome had an
unadjusted rate ratio of 2.15, which was lower than the corresponding
estimates for 6 of the 11 controls. Across the negative controls,
greater baseline imbalance was associated with larger post-index HRs
(r=0.66, p=0.026). The empirical calibration model yielded a predicted
detection-only HR of 1.04 (95% CI 0.37--2.90); importantly, this
interval included the observed seizure HR.

Adjustment for each of six measures of pre-index healthcare contact
moved all 66 negative-control estimates toward the null (median change
−29%; range −46% to −3%). In contrast, the seizure HR increased under
each measure, ranging from 2.46 to 2.93. Because adjustment for
healthcare utilization can have complex causal interpretations, these
findings are supportive but do not establish that differential detection
was absent.

In a separate analysis that adjusted for post-index contact, the seizure
HR fell to 1.25 (95% CI 0.85--1.85). The same adjustment produced
apparent protective associations for fracture and upper respiratory
infection, illustrating the potential for collider bias when
conditioning on post-exposure healthcare contact (eTable 6).

The E-value for the primary HR was 3.99, and the E-value for the lower
confidence limit was 2.62. Emergency department contact had a rate ratio
of 5.74 for IIH versus comparators, exceeding the point-estimate
E-value; however, the corresponding association between emergency
department contact and seizure ascertainment could not be quantified.
The E-value therefore does not exclude detection bias.

# 4 \| DISCUSSION

In this matched electronic health record cohort, patients with IIH had
approximately twice the rate of a clinically recognized incident seizure
or epilepsy event compared with matched comparators (HR 2.28). The
absolute difference was modest: the estimated 3-year cumulative
incidence was 3.83% with IIH versus 1.71% without IIH. The association
persisted across several alternative outcome definitions, cohort
restrictions, follow-up landmarks, and analytic approaches.

The principal challenge in interpreting this association is
ascertainment. Patients with IIH had substantially more healthcare
contact before and after the index date, and every negative-control
outcome was more common at baseline. This makes differential detection a
real concern rather than a theoretical limitation.

Three findings argue against differential detection accounting for the
association in full. First, adjustment for pre-index healthcare contact moved
all negative-control associations toward the null, whereas the seizure
estimate did not attenuate. Second, the association persisted when
events occurring during the first 30, 90, or 180 days after the washout
were excluded, making an explanation based only on the intensive
diagnostic workup surrounding IIH less likely. Third, although surveillance was higher
in the IIH group in every clinician-initiated setting, it was not higher in
laboratory encounters (rate ratio 1.14), the one setting a clinician does not
initiate, and the association persisted. The primary estimate was also unchanged
by restricting the outcome to epilepsy-specific codes or removing the medication
criterion.

These findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. The
empirical calibration analysis was inconclusive: its confidence interval
for the detection-only HR included the observed estimate. Moreover, the
primary outcome could not be compared using the most seizure-specific
channels of ascertainment---neurology visits, EEG, and
neuroimaging---because those data were not captured equivalently in the
two groups. The higher emergency department utilization in the IIH group
is particularly relevant because emergency encounters are an important
setting for first seizure evaluation. Thus, the data do not allow
differential detection to be ruled out.

OSA is another plausible contributor. It is nearly twice as prevalent
among patients with IIH, and is itself associated with epilepsy.20 It was
associated with the outcome in this cohort. Adjustment for OSA, hypertension, and PCOS reduced the HR from
2.28 to 2.11, suggesting that these measured comorbidities explain only
part of the association. Residual confounding is possible, particularly
because OSA is frequently underdiagnosed and was identified through
electronic health record coding.

Treatment may also have influenced ascertainment. Acetazolamide and
topiramate have antiseizure activity, and both are used in IIH.21 Their
use could therefore reduce seizure occurrence, alter the decision to
initiate a separate ASM, or change the probability that a seizure is
captured by the medication-based component of the outcome. If anything,
such treatment effects could attenuate the observed association,
although the direction and magnitude cannot be established from these
data.

Our findings add a population-level perspective to the literature
connecting IIH, temporal encephaloceles, and epilepsy. Prior studies
have largely approached the question from the epilepsy side. In a
presurgical series of 22 patients with drug-resistant temporal lobe
epilepsy associated with anterior temporal meningoencephaloceles, Urbach
and colleagues reported imaging features suggestive of IIH and obesity
in 10 patients.22 Martinez-Poles and colleagues similarly found smaller
pituitary height and larger optic nerve sheath diameters among patients
with temporal pole encephaloceles compared with controls with other
focal epilepsies.23 Case reports have described seizures as a presenting
or predominant manifestation of presumed IIH in patients with skull-base
encephaloceles.24,25 These studies support a biologically plausible link
but cannot estimate seizure risk among patients with IIH.

The mechanism underlying the association remains uncertain. Skull-base
encephaloceles are an attractive candidate because they provide a
structural pathway linking chronically raised intracranial pressure to
focal cortical dysfunction and epilepsy.26--30 Diffuse effects of
sustained pressure, as well as shared metabolic and sleep-related risk
factors, are other possibilities. Encephalocele status was assessed in only 60
patients with IIH, of whom 43 were positive; it was not extracted for
comparators. This precludes meaningful analysis of the structural hypothesis. Future studies should therefore incorporate systematic review
of skull-base imaging and detailed seizure phenotyping rather than
relying on diagnosis codes alone.

The clinical implications are limited by the modest absolute risk and
the uncertainty surrounding ascertainment. These data do not provide a
basis for routine EEG screening or prophylactic ASM treatment in
patients with IIH. They do support careful evaluation of unexplained
paroxysmal events in patients with IIH, particularly when the semiology
is compatible with focal seizures. Conversely, when a patient with
obesity presents with a new focal seizure, review of brain imaging for
subtle signs of raised intracranial pressure and skull-base
encephaloceles may be useful.26,27 When both conditions are present,
treatment selection may require consideration of weight and IIH
management; for example, topiramate may address both seizure risk and
weight-related concerns, whereas some ASMs associated with weight gain
may be less attractive.31 These clinical considerations require
prospective evaluation.

## 4.1 \| Limitations

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, differential ascertainment
could not be excluded. The data elements most specific to seizure
evaluation---neurology encounters, neuroimaging, and EEG---were not
comparably available in the two groups. The comparator cohort was drawn
from the general clinical population rather than from a condition with
similar neurologic surveillance. An active-comparator design may reduce
this concern, although selecting an appropriate comparator would require
careful consideration of how surveillance differs across candidate
conditions.

Second, two eligibility steps were applied asymmetrically. Comparators, but not
cases, were excluded for a history of ischemic stroke, intracerebral or
subarachnoid hemorrhage, craniotomy, brain tumor, or traumatic brain injury,
because the corresponding diagnosis extract was unavailable for the IIH group.
Because each of these conditions raises seizure risk, removing them from the
comparator group alone would be expected to lower the comparator event rate and
bias the hazard ratio away from the null; the magnitude cannot be estimated
without the corresponding extract for the IIH group, and a symmetric
re-analysis is the first priority for any revision. Separately, comparators were
screened for post-washout follow-up at selection, whereas cases were not
subjected to the same step. Restriction to complete matched sets reduced
the HR from 2.28 to 2.06, indicating that this design feature had a
measurable effect, although it did not eliminate the association.

Third, IIH was identified using diagnosis codes and lumbar puncture
rather than criterion-based adjudication. Although restriction to
patients with an opening pressure of at least 25 cm H₂O produced a
similar estimate, opening pressure was available for only 74% of cases.
The primary outcome was also based on codes and medication records.
Seizure type, etiology, and provoked versus unprovoked status were
unavailable, so the outcome cannot be mapped directly onto ILAE
definitions of epilepsy. The recurrence definition is a proxy rather
than a formal epilepsy diagnosis.

Fourth, chart validation was limited to 50 events per group.
False-positive outcomes were slightly more common among patients with
IIH (4% vs 0%), and correcting for this difference would be expected to
attenuate the association modestly, but the small validation sample
limits precision. Encephalocele data were too sparse to test the most
specific structural mechanism.

Fifth, medication capture differed between the two source extracts: the
case extract included inpatient administrations, whereas the comparator
extract contained outpatient prescriptions. This difference could bias
the medication-based component of the outcome toward the null.
Consistent with this possibility, removal of the medication criterion
yielded an HR of 1.93.

Finally, race was unavailable for cases, smoking was not comparably
recorded, and median follow-up was shorter among comparators. Censoring
based on last observed clinical contact may also be informative because
healthcare utilization is related to outcome detection. The study was
conducted within a single integrated academic health system, and the
findings may not generalize to community populations or healthcare
systems with different patterns of surveillance.

# 5 \| CONCLUSIONS

In this matched electronic health record cohort, IIH was associated with
approximately twice the rate of clinically recognized incident seizure
or epilepsy, corresponding to an absolute 3-year excess of about 2
percentage points. Differential healthcare surveillance was present
and cannot be excluded as a partial contributor, but the available evidence
argues against it explaining the association in full. The findings support further investigation in
cohorts with comparable neurologic surveillance, systematic
characterization of seizure phenotype, and dedicated assessment of
skull-base encephaloceles.
