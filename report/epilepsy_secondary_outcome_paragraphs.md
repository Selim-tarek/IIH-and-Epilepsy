# Secondary outcome: epilepsy versus a single seizure

## Results

**Epilepsy versus a single seizure.** We distinguished recurrent unprovoked
seizures (epilepsy) from a single seizure event using three sources of
evidence applied identically in both arms: two or more coded seizure events at
least 30 days apart; an epilepsy-specific ICD code (G40.x or 345.x) or a
descriptor denoting established disease; or antiseizure medication continued
for 180 days or longer. Because recurrence can only be observed after the
first event, ascertainment for this outcome ran from the index date to the
last attended encounter, death, or the data freeze, rather than being censored
at the seizure. Epilepsy occurred in 60 of 2,618 patients with IIH (2.29%,
95% CI 1.75-2.94) and in 63 of 9,122 matched comparators (0.69%, 95% CI
0.53-0.88), corresponding to rates of 4.7 and 1.8 per 1,000 person-years and a
hazard ratio of 2.76 (95% CI 1.95-3.92, p = 1.2 x 10^-8). Restricting to
patients with at least one encounter in the 12 months before index, as
specified in the protocol, gave 2.30% versus 0.40% and a hazard ratio of 3.87
(95% CI 2.23-6.73). Among the 102 patients with IIH who had an incident
seizure, 60 (58.8%) met criteria for epilepsy and 42 (41.2%) had a single
seizure event without recorded recurrence. Documented recurrence was the
commonest qualifying channel (53 patients), followed by an epilepsy-specific
code (31) and chronic antiseizure medication (6); the channels overlap. Eleven
patients in total, six with IIH and five comparators, had antiseizure
medication continued for 180 days or more, most commonly levetiracetam, with
individual treatment spans of up to 3,631 days.

**Ascertainment of the comparator arm.** The proportion of outcome-positive
patients meeting epilepsy criteria was 63 of 64 (98.4%) in the comparator arm,
against 60 of 102 (58.8%) in the IIH arm. We do not interpret this contrast as
a difference in disease severity. Only one comparator in the entire cohort of
9,122 was classified as a single seizure without recurrence, and none in the
engagement-restricted cohort, which is implausible for a population of that
size; a single unprovoked seizure is common and would be expected in dozens of
individuals. A parallel examination of the diagnosis and problem-list extracts
identified 194 comparators carrying qualifying seizure codes within their own
follow-up window who were not classified as outcome-positive, against none in
the IIH arm. Taken together these observations indicate that seizures at the
milder end of the spectrum were captured less completely in comparators than
in patients with IIH, so that the comparator outcome group is enriched for
established epilepsy. We therefore report the epilepsy outcome on the cohort
denominator, where both numerator and denominator are defined identically in
the two arms, and do not report the within-outcome proportions as a
comparison.

## Discussion

**Interpretation.** Distinguishing epilepsy from a single seizure matters
clinically, because the two carry different implications for driving,
occupation, pregnancy planning, and long-term antiseizure treatment. In this
cohort the excess associated with IIH was not confined to isolated events: the
rate of recurrent or established epilepsy was approximately three times higher
in patients with IIH than in matched comparators, and the estimate was of
similar magnitude in the engagement-restricted analysis. Among patients with
IIH who did have a seizure, a majority went on to recurrent disease, which is
the more useful figure when counselling an individual patient who has just
presented with a first event. These findings should not be read causally. The
analysis is observational, the outcome is derived from routinely collected
records rather than from adjudicated chart review, and residual confounding by
factors that independently raise both the likelihood of an IIH diagnosis and
the likelihood of a seizure diagnosis cannot be excluded.

**Limitations specific to this outcome.** Three limitations bear directly on
how much weight the epilepsy result can carry. First, ascertainment of
seizures was less complete in comparators than in patients with IIH, as
described above; because the missed events appear to be the milder ones, the
comparator epilepsy count is best regarded as a lower bound, and the true
hazard ratio may be smaller than the estimate reported here. Quantifying that
gap requires chart review of a sample of the discordant comparators, which we
were not able to undertake. Second, the medication channel is not comparable
between the arms. Antiseizure medication records were available for 17 of 102
outcome-positive patients with IIH but for 48 of 64 comparators, and the agent
most frequently recorded among cases was fosphenytoin, an intravenous drug
used acutely, reflecting a source that captures inpatient administrations for
cases and outpatient prescriptions for comparators. Chronic outpatient therapy
in the IIH arm is therefore substantially under-captured, and the eleven
patients identified as receiving indefinite treatment should be read as a
floor rather than as a count. Topiramate, gabapentin and clobazam were
excluded from the medication definition because topiramate is first-line
therapy for IIH itself and gabapentin is an analgesic, so that including
either would have generated epilepsy classifications in the IIH arm by
construction. Third, the requirement of two coded events at least 30 days
apart, or of an epilepsy-specific code, will misclassify as single seizures
those patients whose recurrences were managed outside the contributing health
system. This misclassification is expected to operate in both arms, but it is
likely to be more pronounced in comparators, whose engagement with the system
was lower by design.
